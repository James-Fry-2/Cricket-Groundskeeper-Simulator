# Cricket Groundskeeping — Research Notes

Reference document for a future game about being a cricket curator / groundskeeper.
Compiled Aug 2026. Sources: Grounds Management Association (UK), NZC pitch prep manual,
Sports Turf WA, BCCI, ESPNcricinfo, ICC pitch rating reports.

---

## 1. The job in one line

A curator (Aus/India) or groundsman/head groundsperson (UK) manufactures a strip of
compacted clay soil that has to behave predictably for up to five days, under weather
they don't control, for two teams who want opposite things from it, judged publicly by
match referees, captains, commentators and members.

The often-quoted framing: preparing a pitch is like baking a cake — the steps and the
timing both have to be right, and watering/rolling timing is the hardest part to learn.
It's explicitly described in the trade literature as trial and error for new curators.

---

## 2. Anatomy of the ground

- **The square** — the block of prepared strips in the middle. A club square might hold
  8–12 pitches; the curator rotates through them across a season and repairs used ones.
- **The pitch/wicket** — the single 22-yard strip in use. 20.12m long, 3.05m wide.
- **The outfield** — everything else. Judged on true ball roll and safety of footing;
  rated separately by the ICC.
- **Run-ups** — bowlers' approach areas; covered separately in rain, and the first thing
  to go soft.
- **The soil** — cricket loam, high clay content. Clay is what gives you compaction and
  therefore pace and bounce. Key soil vocabulary: pore space, capillary vs non-capillary
  pores, field capacity (~24h after saturation), saturation, waterlogging.

---

## 3. Core preparation loop (the ten-day prep)

Standard GMA sequence, started 10–14 days out, **never less than 5 days**:

1. Identify the pitch (string-line it off the square)
2. Brush
3. Mow
4. Rake / scarify
5. Irrigate
6. Roll
7. Repeat brush–mow–roll over the remaining days
8. Mark out creases

### Watering
- Wet the top **75–100mm** so the roller can compact rather than skate.
- Water moves slowly through loam — reaching depth can take **a day or more**.
- Applied via travelling sprinkler, soaker hose or hand hose; flat sheet after watering
  slows evaporation.
- Rule of thumb: water deeply 4–5 days out, taper as match day approaches. Target is
  **moisture at depth, dry at the surface**.
- Pros use moisture meters/soil profile tools; ~25–30% moisture in top 100mm is a
  commonly cited working figure.
- Hard-won wisdom: don't put in more water than the weather can take out, especially
  with no covers and an unsettled forecast.

### Rolling
- Start 1–2 days after watering — moist but **never wet to the touch**.
- ~20–30 min/day, tapering as the soil dries. Some schedules go 30–45 min at days 5–3,
  20–30 min at days 2–1, and only a light maintenance roll on match day.
- Roll **lengthwise**, never across.
- Roller escalation: lightest roller first for smoothness → medium for tightness →
  heavy roller last, and **don't overuse the heavy one**. Roller diameter and rolling
  speed both affect final bounce.
- Roll too wet → clay compression/cracking. Roll too dry → no compaction, low slow pitch.

### Mowing
- Cut out around 10mm early; reduce height of cut ~2–4mm per week; final playing height
  roughly **6–8mm**.
- Mow 2–3× per week during prep. Brush or rake before cutting to stand the grass up.
- Goal: evenly grassed with **some soil visible** — not a matted carpet.
- Grass left on = seam movement. The MCG's infamous 2025 Boxing Day strip had 10mm left on.

**Failure state to model:** incorrect prep → slow, low pitch with erratic bounce.

## 4. What the curator is actually tuning

| Lever | More of it gives you |
|---|---|
| Rolling | Hardness → pace and consistent bounce; favours batters |
| Water at depth | Bounce and carry; life for quicks |
| Surface dryness | Cracking later, turn for spinners |
| Grass cover | Seam movement, ball preservation, binding |
| Soil clay content | Potential hardness, but also cracking |
| Time | Everything — you can't rush compaction |

Format shapes the brief: T20 wants a batting track; ODI wants ~30–40% in it for the
bowlers; a Test pitch ideally has some moisture day one and evolves across five days.

## 5. The annual calendar (Northern Hemisphere shape; invert for Aus/NZ/SA)

- **Sept–Oct — End-of-season renovation.** The foundation of next year. Aims: remove
  organic matter/thatch, restore surface levels, repair grass cover. Scarify hard,
  aerate, overseed, top-dress with matching loam, pre-seed/autumn fertiliser. Every
  pitch gets renovated, used or not. Skipping it is false economy — costs compound.
- **Nov–Feb — Winter.** Drag-brush frequently, keep grass topped ~25mm to avoid disease,
  solid-tine aerate when moisture allows, maintain fencing against intruders (foxes,
  dogs, shortcut-takers), service machinery.
- **Mar–Apr — Pre-season rolling.** Slowly re-compact from the bottom up. Deep gradual
  compaction, not quick surface compaction that breaks up in June.
- **May–Aug — In season.** Prep pitches in a planned sequence, repair immediately after
  a match so strips can be reused, square mown ~2×/week, outfield ~1×/week, slow-release
  fertiliser, light aeration, increased brushing in late summer as dew brings disease.
- **August** — order renovation materials now; strips coming out of use can be scarified
  early for a head start.

**Effort reality (UK premier-standard 12-pitch club, per Cheshire AoG):** ~210 hrs
outfield, ~100 hrs pitch prep/repair, ~105 hrs auxiliary (covers, sightscreens, hedges),
~40 hrs renovation. ~1,155 hrs total, ~£30k machinery investment, ~£38k/yr all-in.
Good economic model for a management-sim budget layer.

## 6. Weather and the emergency layer

- **Covers.** Flat sheets, wheeled/hover covers, whole-field sheets. Poor drainage
  usually means you cover more. Wind is the enemy — Galle's crew weighs covers down with
  old truck tyres.
- **Speed matters.** Galle's ground staff, split into four crews working like an F1 pit
  crew, get ~80% of the field covered in under nine minutes.
- **Super Sopper.** Rolling sponge invented by Gordon Withnall in Australia, 1974, after
  his golf ball landed in a puddle. Takes ~40–45 min to clear a cricket field. It can run
  out of fuel and break down — this actually happened at the 2024 T20 World Cup in
  Lauderhill.
- **Drainage tech tiers.** Basic slope → modern sand-carpet → sub-air vacuum systems
  (Chinnaswamy, Bengaluru: ~15 min turnaround, ~10,000 litres/min). Great as an
  upgrade tree.
- **Umpires, not the curator, decide fitness for play** — player safety is their call.
  You can only present the ground.

## 7. Judgement, politics and pressure

- **ICC ratings** after every international: *very good / satisfactory (good) /
  unsatisfactory / unfit*. Match referee assesses, consulting the two captains.
- **Demerits:** unsatisfactory = 1 point, unfit = 3. Points live on a rolling five-year
  window; accumulate enough (reported thresholds of 5–6) and the venue is banned from
  international cricket for 12 months, 24 for worse.
- Outfields are rated separately — Kanpur 2024 took a demerit for the outfield while the
  pitch itself rated satisfactory.
- **Case study — MCG, Boxing Day 2025.** 36 wickets in 142 overs, Test over in two days,
  no batter reached 50 (first time in Australia in 73 years). Referee Jeff Crowe:
  too much in favour of the bowlers. Head curator Matt Page said he was in a state of
  shock. The club publicly backed him; the coach warned against boards dictating
  tailor-made surfaces. Page had earlier been poached from the WACA precisely because
  the MCG was too dull — it got a "poor" rating in 2017 for a lifeless draw.
- **Case study — Newlands 2024.** Shortest result Test in history, 642 balls. Both
  captains agreed the surface was below standard; unsatisfactory, one demerit.
- The recurring theme: **the curator is squeezed between the home team wanting an
  advantage, the board wanting a spectacle, the broadcaster wanting five days, and the
  ICC wanting a fair contest.** Australian curators have traditionally been given
  independence with a brief to preserve each venue's "unique character."

## 8. Game design hooks

**Core loop:** read forecast → allocate limited daily labour hours across watering,
rolling, mowing, brushing, outfield, repairs → sleep → weather resolves → pitch state
drifts → match day → pitch performs → get rated.

**Hidden-state tension.** The player never sees true soil moisture; they see proxies —
a soil profile core, a moisture meter (upgradeable, more precise), the feel of the
surface, footprints. Uncertainty is the game.

**Resources:** labour hours, staff (permanent + casual, cf. Galle's tuk-tuk driver
earning ~$7.44/day), water, loam, seed, fuel, budget, machinery condition.

**Machinery as characters:** light/medium/heavy rollers, reel mower, groomer kit,
scarifier, aerator, Super Sopper (can break), covers, sprinkler system. Repair and
upgrade tree.

**Failure modes worth simulating:** thatch build-up from skipped renovation, uneven
bounce from inconsistent rolling/moisture, excessive cracking from clay drying too fast,
a strip re-used too soon, waterlogging, covers blowing off in wind.

**Multi-season stakes:** renovation quality this September silently sets the ceiling for
next June. Long-horizon consequences are the strongest hook here.

**Antagonists / pressure sources:** captain wanting turn, board wanting a five-day gate,
groundsman's own reputation, ICC demerit ledger, members complaining, weather.

**Scoring:** rate the pitch the way the ICC does — did it produce an even contest? Not
"did your team win." A player who bakes a bunsen to win a Test can still eat a demerit.

**Setting variety:** village club (volunteers, one roller, no covers) → county/state
ground → Test venue (staff of 20, sub-air drainage, live TV). Also regional identity:
WACA pace, Galle turn, English seam, subcontinental dust.
